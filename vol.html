How to read it. Each slice across strike is a Hagan SABR smile. Negative ρ tilts it so that downside strikes trade rich, which is the usual shape in equity and crude oil. ν curves the wings. Vol-of-vol usually falls with expiry, which is why the decay term exists: long-dated smiles flatten even though ρ is the same. This is the parameterisation I calibrated across 20+ exchange-traded commodity option surfaces at Deloitte.

Why the density matters. The second derivative of call prices with respect to strike, scaled by erT, gives the market-implied distribution. A good-looking smile can still give a negative density in the wings. That is static arbitrage, and the Hagan expansion is known to produce it at low strikes when ν·√T is large, so I check it before trusting any calibrated surface.

Synthetic quotes

The sidebar parameters define the "true" smile at the focus expiry. Quotes are drawn from it with Gaussian noise. β is fixed, and α, ρ and ν are fitted by Nelder–Mead on transformed parameters (log α, atanh ρ, log ν), starting from an ATM-anchored guess.

What the scatter shows. ρ and ν trade off against each other: both bend the wings, so noisy wing quotes move them together along a ridge. That is why a desk fixes β, anchors α to ATM, and watches parameter stability from day to day instead of trusting one fit.
